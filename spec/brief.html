This is the whole idea. One row per person, one column per day, a mark when they showed up. You don't read it, you see it: Dave never misses, Mia only shows up on weekends, Jo tried it for four days and vanished, Nova just caught fire.

The problem

Founders have analytics. They still can't answer "is this working?"

A founder needs three things every week: who is actually using the product, whether the business is on track, and what's coming. Today that means four tools, none of which answer the question, plus a spreadsheet somebody updates by hand on Sunday night.

Averages
Product analytics tools were built for analysts running queries. They tell you "daily actives: 1,240." They cannot tell you whether Dave came back. You end up staring at a line chart that has averaged away every person inside it.
No business review
The Amazon weekly business review is the gold standard for running a company on numbers, and there is essentially one open-source implementation of it in existence. Everyone else runs their week off a hand-updated spreadsheet.
Nothing connects
Revenue lives in one tool, users in another, releases in a third, payroll in the bank, the roadmap in a doc. The founder is the integration layer, copying numbers between tabs to assemble one picture, every single week.
Built for mice
Founders now work alongside AI agents all day. Every one of these tools assumes a human with a mouse. An agent asking "who churned this week, and why?" has no good way in, and no way to show its work.
Analytics tools show you numbers. Founders need to see people. The best-known method for finding product-market fit is a chart drawn by hand: one row per named user, one column per day, a dot when they used the product. You look at it and you can feel retention — who is sticking, who vanished, who came back after a month away. Founders draw this in spreadsheets because no tool ships it well. It is the most useful artifact in early-stage analytics and it is the one thing nobody builds.
The solution

One dashboard, built around that chart

ANYKPI is a single open-source dashboard with five sections. It doesn't collect your data — your existing tools stay the source of truth. It quietly pulls summaries from each of them on a schedule, keeps a fast local copy, and shows an honest "synced 4 minutes ago" on every source. It never writes anything back to a connected tool. ANYKPI reads.

Everything is built on one principle, and it is what makes the product feel different within the first thirty seconds:

The answer is the view. Ask a question — typed by you, or asked by your agent — and you don't get a paragraph explaining the answer. You land on the screen that is the answer, filtered to exactly the people or numbers in question, with a link you can paste into the group chat. Ask for "iOS users in France" and you arrive at the plot with two removable filter chips already applied. No summary strip on top narrating what just happened.
What's in it

Five sections

Dot Plot

who's actually using it

Every user gets a row, every day a column, and runs of activity merge into continuous strips so streaks and silences read instantly without counting anything. Group people by behaviour, by the week they signed up, or by company account. Zoom from a year down to a single day. Drag a thumbnail map to travel across hundreds of users and months of history. Hover anyone for their card: who they are, what they're worth, what they've been doing.

Milestones appear as stickers on the day they happened — first payment 💸, signup birthday 🎂, went quiet 👻.

Cohorts

is it working

The retention triangle and the decay curves, linked both ways — hover a number and its curve lights up. The system watches for the shape that actually matters: a curve that flattens instead of falling to zero, meaning a group of people who simply keep coming back. When enough groups flatten, it says so plainly: product-market fit is forming.

This is the one moment the product celebrates. There is confetti. It tells you to go tell the group chat.

Weekly Business Review

is the business on track

The Amazon method, kept intact: every metric shown as six trailing weeks beside twelve trailing months, this year against last year, inputs separated from outputs and ordered so the deck reads as a causal argument. The system finds its own exceptions and writes the sentence explaining each one — "six weeks under target, and still falling." Walk the deck, focus a single metric with its full data sheet, or see all of them in one table.

21 metrics across finance, acquisition, activation, retention and quality — surfacing roughly three to discuss and two to watch in a normal week.

Calendar

what's coming

Read-only, permanently and by design. It never asks you to enter anything. It lines up what your connected tools already know — payouts, renewals, releases, payroll, board meetings — alongside milestones it detects on its own, like your 1,000th signup or the company's birthday. The past renders as record, the future as projection, and every card shows exactly which source it came from.

PMF+

who these people actually are

Point at one user or a whole group and say "go understand these people." A researcher goes and finds them across the public internet, reads their actual content, and comes back with a card: who they are, what they're into, what their behaviour in your product means given who they are, and two or three sharp questions worth asking that specific person. It can draft the outreach too, each message waiting in a queue for your approval.

Nothing ever sends on its own. That is a permanent property of the product, not a setting.

Running underneath all five: type a question in plain English and land in the view that answers it. Your agents get the same access you do, and every answer they give comes back with a link to the view that proves it.

Requirements

The build plan

Who it's for. Seed to Series A founders and small product teams who already pay for product analytics, still can't answer "is this working?", and who work with AI agents every day. Secondary audience: the open-source community that adopts tools a founder can run without a data team.

Where it stands. The prototype is finished and verified in a real browser — all five sections, ten rounds of design fixes, every decision recorded. That prototype is the specification. The production architecture is written and approved. What remains is building it against real data.

Decisions of record

The calendar is read-only forever. No event editor, no manual entry, anywhere. It is a lens over what the company already runs on, never a place to keep things.

No self-narrating chrome. The product never explains what it just did. No summary strips, no suggestion chips, no methodology quotes. The view is the answer.

Motion is one-shot and earned. No glowing lines, no looping animation, no shadows on data marks. Energy comes from colour and type; playfulness lives in emoji, copy and confetti.

Demo data ships forever. A stranger who installs it never meets an empty screen, and that same dataset is what every automated check runs against.

How "done" is defined

Every phase opens with a page of promises written in plain customer language — the things a person will be able to do — which you approve in about two minutes. That approval is your only review. Nothing is called finished until every promise passes automatically in a real browser. No phase ever ships on someone's word that it works.

Needs your call

Three open decisions

None of them block starting. All three should be settled before anything goes public.

The name Recommendation: keep ANYKPI. It is the working name of record and it is already built into the prototype. Worth settling explicitly whether it sits under your anytime.day brand or stands alone — standalone is easier to hand to an open-source community that may outlive the parent brand.
The license Recommendation: MIT. Maximally permissive, the standard for tools you want widely adopted, and it does not stop you selling a hosted version later. That is the same open-core path Plausible, Cal.com and PostHog all took.
The hosted waitlist link Recommendation: keep it in v1. It is the cheapest possible demand test for a paid hosted version, it costs one line of interface, and every comparable open-source product validated its business exactly this way.
